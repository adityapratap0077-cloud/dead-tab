Cicada 3301: The Puzzle With No Author

Three rounds of the hardest puzzles the public internet has ever produced, signed with a key, claimed by no one.

This is a case file, not a story with an ending, because there is no ending yet. It follows the museum's open-case format exactly: what is established, what others claim, what evidence exists, what is disputed, what else could explain it, and what remains unknown. Established facts

  • 4 Jan 2012 — An image appears on 4chan's /b/ board: "Hello. We are looking for highly intelligent individuals…" Solving the steganography inside it opens the first Cicada 3301 puzzle trail, which runs for about a month and at one point leads to physical posters bearing QR codes in multiple countries.
  • 4 Jan 2013 — A second round begins, exactly a year later. One participant who publicly described winning this round (in Rolling Stone's reporting) said the winners were questioned about their commitment to information freedom and privacy, and invited to collaborate on a project in a private forum.
  • 4 Jan 2014 — A third round begins via Twitter. It leads to the Liber Primus, a book written in runes, of which only a minority has been publicly deciphered. The third puzzle remains unsolved.
  • Throughout — Genuine Cicada messages were signed with the same OpenPGP key, whose fingerprint ends in 7A35090F. That signature, not the cicada image or the number, is the only accepted test of authenticity.
  • 5 Jan 2016 — A new signed clue points solvers back to the Liber Primus and warns against false paths.
  • Apr 2017 — The last verified signed message appears: "Beware false paths. Always verify PGP signature from 7A35090F." It explicitly denies the validity of any unsigned puzzle.
  • Since 2017 — No verified message, no new genuine round, and no party, company, agency, or individual has ever credibly claimed authorship.

Claims made by others

The following are claims in circulation, recorded because a case file should show its weather. None is supported by verified evidence, and Wikipedia's summary of the speculation says the same:

  • That Cicada 3301 was a recruitment instrument for an intelligence agency (the NSA, CIA, MI6, and Mossad are all named in different versions of the claim).
  • That it was a Masonic or secret-society selection ritual, or a cult's filter for initiates.
  • That it was a cyber-mercenary group's hiring test.
  • That it was an alternate reality game that simply never monetised.
  • Various unsigned "new Cicada puzzles" and "winners' emails" that circulate periodically. Under the standard the organisers themselves published in 2017, every unsigned item is by definition not genuine, and this museum treats them as impostor material.

Available evidence

The puzzle artifacts themselves: the three trails' images, texts, and solutions are extensively documented by solver communities and remain inspectable. The PGP signature chain is publicly verifiable and internally consistent across 2012 to 2017, which establishes that one continuous party (or keyholder) ran the genuine rounds. The Liber Primus exists as a concrete artifact: a runic text whose deciphered portions are philosophical in character, and whose remainder is a standing, checkable challenge. Contemporary reporting, including The Washington Post's description of Cicada as one of the "top 5 eeriest, unsolved mysteries of the Internet", documents the phenomenon's public footprint at the time.

Disputed information

Details of the winners' experience rest largely on a small number of self-reported accounts; they are consistent with each other but not independently verified. Counts of the Liber Primus (its page total, and how many pages have been solved) differ between sources; this file therefore says only that a minority has been deciphered. Whether anyone completed the 2012 round's final stages in the way the organisers intended is itself not publicly established.

Plausible alternative explanations

  • A recruitment filter, but a private one: a small security firm, research group, or collective hiring by ordeal rather than by advertisement. Fits the stated intent and the winners' accounts; requires no state actor.
  • An art or philosophy project: the Liber Primus's content and the absence of any monetisation or follow-through fit a group whose goal was the work itself.
  • A cypherpunk demonstration: a proof, staged at global scale, that anonymous coordination and strong cryptography work. The 2017 sign-off, concerned only with signature hygiene, reads naturally in this frame.
  • An intelligence-agency exercise: possible, and the explanation most often printed; but no evidence has surfaced for it in over a decade, and agencies that recruit by puzzle now do so openly, under their own names.

What remains unknown · Editorial conclusion

Unknown: who Cicada 3301 was; what the winners, if any completed the path, actually built or joined; the full text and meaning of the Liber Primus; and whether the silence since April 2017 is an ending or an interval.

Conclusion, as strong as the evidence allows: a single organised party ran three sophisticated, signed puzzle rounds between 2012 and 2014, directed winners toward values of privacy and information freedom, left an unfinished book, and stopped talking. Everything beyond that sentence is speculation, and this case stays open precisely where the evidence stops. This museum does not attempt to identify the people behind Cicada 3301, and asks its readers not to either: the right to be an unsolved exhibit is part of what the early internet was for.
