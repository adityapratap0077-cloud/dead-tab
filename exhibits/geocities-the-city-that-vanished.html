GeoCities: The City That Vanished

Before the feed, the web had neighbourhoods, and millions of ordinary people built the houses.

If Orkut was a place Indians lived socially, GeoCities was where the whole early web lived domestically: the first mass experience of having an address on the internet that was yours. It is also this museum's founding parable, because its death in 2009 produced the most dramatic rescue in internet archiving history.

Homesteaders

GeoCities began in November 1994, founded by David Bohnett and John Rezner and briefly named Beverly Hills Internet. Its organising idea was a city: free web pages were plots of land in themed neighbourhoods named for real places, technology in SiliconValley, entertainment in Hollywood, and dozens more for everything from conservation to military history. Residents were called homesteaders, and they built accordingly: guestbooks, visitor counters, midi music, tiled backgrounds, and the immortal "under construction" sign on pages that were, in a sense, always under construction and always finished.

It worked at a scale that is hard to overstate. By 1998 GeoCities was among the three most popular websites in the world, with more than 19 million users. On 28 January 1999, Yahoo announced it would acquire the company in a stock deal valued at about $3.6 billion; at that moment GeoCities hosted some 3.5 million member sites. The neighbourhood addresses were retired that July in favour of member-name URLs, and the city slowly became a suburb of a portal.

The notice on the door

In April 2009 Yahoo announced the US service would close, and stopped new registrations. The closure date was 26 October 2009. What was being demolished was not a failed startup: reporting at the time put the still-standing city at the 218th most visited destination on the internet, with at least 38 million user-written pages. Fan shrines, family histories, small-business pages, poetry, homework help, entire subcultures' first drafts: the ordinary written record of the web's first mass decade, scheduled for deletion because it no longer fit a strategy.

Running through a burning building

The rescue is the reason this museum exists in spirit. The volunteer Archive Team, coordinated by digital historian Jason Scott, spent the six months between announcement and shutdown downloading as much of GeoCities as automated scripts could reach, after Yahoo declined to provide any list of what existed. "It was like running through a burning building and deciding what to save," Scott said. About 100,000 accounts were captured in the effort Computerworld documented, and parallel projects, Reocities, OoCities, geocities.ws and others, saved overlapping pieces of the city. A year after the shutdown, Archive Team released its harvest as a torrent of roughly 650 gigabytes, so the archive would live on thousands of machines instead of one company's mercy.

The official closure date and the observed one differ by a day: Archive Team's own record notes the pages actually went dark around midday Pacific on 27 October 2009, with stragglers lingering for days. Even the death certificate of a city is approximate. GeoCities Japan, a separate service, kept its lights on until 31 March 2019.

  • Nov 1994 — Founded as Beverly Hills Internet; renamed GeoCities.
  • 1998 — Among the world's three most popular sites; 19M+ users.
  • 28 Jan 1999 — Yahoo acquisition announced, ~$3.6B in stock; 3.5M hosted sites.
  • Apr 2009 — Closure announced; registrations stop.
  • 26 Oct 2009 — GeoCities US shuts down with at least 38M pages (pages observed going dark on the 27th).
  • 2010 — Archive Team releases the rescued data as a ~650 GB torrent.
  • 31 Mar 2019 — GeoCities Japan closes, a decade later.

Evidence and uncertainties

Well sourced: founding date and original name, the neighbourhood model, the acquisition date and valuation (contemporary reporting), the closure announcement and date, the scale at shutdown, and the Archive Team rescue effort.

Handle with care: torrent size is reported as 642 GB, 652 GB, and "a terabyte" by different outlets; this exhibit says "roughly 650 gigabytes". What fraction of GeoCities was ultimately saved is unknown, including to the rescuers; any source claiming a precise percentage should be distrusted. One later outlet gives a 1996 founding date, contradicted by the stronger sources, and is not followed here.

The lesson: GeoCities is the proof that preservation cannot be left to owners. Everything in this museum's method descends from what Archive Team did in 2009.
