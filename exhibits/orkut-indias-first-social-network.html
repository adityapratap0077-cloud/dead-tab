Orkut: India's First Social Network

A Google side project that two enormous countries turned into their social home, and then watched being switched off.

Every Indian who was a teenager in the late 2000s has an Orkut story, and most of them begin the same way: somebody's scrapbook. Orkut was not the first social website Indians used, but it was the first one that felt like a place, with regulars, reputations, and rituals. This exhibit documents its rise, its strange geography, and its clean, instructive death.

A side project with a country's name on it

Orkut was built by Google engineer Orkut Büyükkökten as an independent project inside Google, and the site carried his first name. It launched in late January 2004; sources disagree on the exact day, with Wikipedia's own history section and infobox giving 22 and 24 January respectively, and this exhibit declines to pretend otherwise. Facebook launched days later, in February 2004. The race that defined social media began, in other words, as a photo finish that the photo finish's loser won for a decade in the places this museum cares about.

Scraps, testimonials, and the performance of friendship

Orkut's grammar was distinctive. You left scraps: public messages on a friend's profile, visible to everyone, which made conversation itself a performance. Friends wrote testimonials for each other, formal little certificates of affection that read like yearbook entries and were treasured accordingly. You could rate friends as Trustworthy, Cool, or Sexy, maintain a Crush List, and join communities for anything: a college, a city bus route, a shared hatred of Monday. Profile themes were offered only in Brazil and India, a small settings-menu confession of where the site's real population lived.

Google noticed. In August 2007 a redesign launched Orkut in six Indian languages: Hindi, Bengali, Marathi, Tamil, Kannada, and Telugu. In 2008, with Orkut among the most visited websites in both India and Brazil, Google moved its management to Belo Horizonte, Brazil. The traffic numbers tell the story of a complete inversion: in March 2004 the United States supplied over half of Orkut's visits; by the shutdown, Brazil was 55.5 percent and India 18.4 percent, with the US down to 3.3 percent.

The long overtaking

Facebook passed Orkut in India in September 2010, when Orkut still counted a reported 19 million Indian users; Brazil, the last stronghold, followed around the turn of 2012. On 30 June 2014 Google announced the end, citing the faster growth of its other communities. On 30 September 2014, Orkut shut down.

The shutdown is why this exhibit sits in a museum rather than an obituary column. Users could export their photo albums before the end, and profiles, scraps, testimonials, and community posts stayed exportable through Google Takeout until September 2016. Most unusually, Google kept the public content of public communities online as a read-only Community Archive: an official ruin, preserved by the same company that demolished the city. A decade of friendship, in other words, survived on a two-year export window and one act of corporate mercy.

The afterlife

In April 2022, orkut.com came back from the dead carrying only a letter from Büyükkökten promising that something new was being built, framed around healthier communities. As of October 2026 reporting, a supporter campaign for a revival exists and the founder has spoken about regaining the brand's rights in 2025. There is no relaunched service and no launch date; this museum records the campaign as a campaign, not a resurrection.

  • Jan 2004 — Orkut launches at Google (exact day disputed: 22 vs 24 January).
  • Aug 2007 — Redesign adds six Indian languages.
  • 2008 — Management moves to Google Brazil, Belo Horizonte.
  • Sep 2010 — Facebook overtakes Orkut in India.
  • 30 Jun 2014 — Google announces the shutdown.
  • 30 Sep 2014 — Orkut closes; the Community Archive remains online, read-only.
  • Apr 2022 — orkut.com reactivates with the founder's letter.

Evidence and uncertainties

Well sourced: the shutdown and announcement dates, the Takeout window, the Community Archive, the 2007 Indian-language launch, the 2008 move to Brazil, and the traffic inversion (Alexa data as compiled by Wikipedia).

Handle with care: the famous "300 million users" figure traces to the founder's own letter and secondary retellings, not to an independent measurement, so this exhibit does not use it as fact. The exact January 2004 launch day is disputed within our main source itself. Month-level dates for Facebook's Brazil overtake differ by a few months between sources.

Recollection: what a testimonial meant to a fifteen-year-old is not in any of these sources. That part belongs to our readers, and the submission page is open.
