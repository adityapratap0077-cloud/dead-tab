## EXHIBIT 002 — ORKUT

### Verified timeline

- **January 2004 (launch; exact day disputed by sources)** — Orkut was launched by Google employee Orkut Büyükkökten, who built it as an independent/"20 percent" project at Google, and the site was named after him. Wikipedia's History section says it was "quietly launched on **January 22, 2004**," while Wikipedia's own infobox lists the founding date as **January 24, 2004**. Both dates circulate; the exhibit should say "launched in late January 2004" or present the two dates openly. (Wikipedia, "Orkut"; rip.so also gives 22 January 2004.)
- **2004–2008 (features and character)** — Core features included profiles, friend lists, **scrapbooks** (public messages left on a friend's profile — "scraps"), **communities** (interest groups; Ning was considered a direct competitor on this), friend ratings ("Trustworthy", "Cool", "Sexy" on a 1–3 scale, aggregated as percentages), the ability to become a "fan" of a friend, a "Crush List", polls, and profile themes (themes were only available in Brazil and India). Orkut was the first Google product to support OpenSocial. (Wikipedia, "Orkut", Features section.)
- **2008** — Orkut was one of the most visited websites in India and Brazil. That year Google announced Orkut would be fully managed and operated by Google Brazil in Belo Horizonte, explicitly because of the large Brazilian user base and legal issues. (Wikipedia, "Orkut".)
- **31 August 2007** — A redesign added, among other things, release in six new languages: Hindi, Bengali, Marathi, Tamil, Kannada and Telugu; on 4 September 2007 an "Updates from your friends" box launched, and scraps became HTML-enabled. (Wikipedia, "Orkut", Redesigns.) *(Date kept because it shows the deliberate India localisation.)*
- **September 2010** — Facebook overtook Orkut in number of users **in India**; Orkut at that point had a reported 19 million users in India and 34 million in Brazil. (The Economic Times, "7 social media sites that failed to become 'Facebook'" — figures as reported there.)
- **January 2012** — Facebook surpassed Orkut in Brazil, its last stronghold (MarTech, "Requiem for Orkut"; The Economic Times says Facebook passed Orkut in Brazil "a year later" than India, i.e. roughly 2011 — sources differ by a few months).
- **30 June 2014** — Google announced Orkut would close on 30 September 2014; no new accounts could be created from July 2014. (Wikipedia, "Orkut".)
- **30 September 2014** — **Orkut shut down.** Users could export photo albums before shutdown; profiles, scraps, **testimonials**, and community posts remained exportable via Google Takeout **until September 2016**. Google engineering director Paulo Golgher's blog post justified the closure on the grounds that other Google communities' growth had outpaced Orkut's. Crucially for the museum: **the public content of all public communities was archived by Google and kept permanently consultable online as the Orkut Community Archive** (read-only). (Wikipedia, "Orkut", Shutdown section.)
- **At shutdown (30 September 2014)** — Traffic by country was Brazil 55.5% and India 18.4%, with the US down to 3.3% — a complete inversion of March 2004, when the US was 51.36% and Brazil 5.16%. (Wikipedia, "Orkut", traffic table, citing Alexa data.)
- **April 2022** — orkut.com was reactivated after years offline, displaying only a letter from founder Orkut Büyükkökten stating that something new was to come; contemporary coverage (Free Press Journal, summarising the 2022 message) describes it as a project to build communities and fight hate and misinformation. (Wikipedia, "Orkut"; Free Press Journal, October 2026.)
- **October 2026 (epilogue, optional)** — orkut.com was updated with a revival supporter campaign; per Wikipedia citing Exame, Büyükkökten said the return became possible after he regained the brand's IP rights in September 2025. This is a campaign to gauge interest, **not a relaunch**, and there is no launch date. (Wikipedia, "Orkut"; Free Press Journal; The Print.)

### Claims NOT verified / handle with care

- **"300 million users"** — Repeated everywhere, including in Büyükkökten's own letter ("Orkut had become a community of over 300 million people") and rip.so ("peak users ~300 million, 2010"). **Flag:** in the sources actually read, this figure traces to the founder and secondary retellings, not to an independent measurement; Wikipedia's verifiable data is country traffic shares, not a global user total. Present as "reported/claimed" or omit.
- **Exact launch date** — January 22 vs January 24, 2004 conflict exists *within* Wikipedia itself. Do not print a single day as certain.
- **"Facebook launched two weeks after Orkut"** (Economic Times) — Facebook's launch is commonly dated 4 February 2004, which is ~13 days after 22 January but only ~11 days after 24 January; "two weeks" is approximate journalism, not a verified interval.

### Why it matters culturally

Orkut was the first social network that much of India and Brazil ever lived in — for millions, "the internet" as a social place began with a scrapbook, a testimonial written by a school friend, and communities named after everything from a bus route to a shared grievance. Its geography was the story: built in California, it was quietly colonised by Brazil and India until Google literally moved its management to Belo Horizonte, localised it into six Indian languages, and eventually buried it in favour of products the rest of the world preferred. The shutdown on 30 September 2014 is an early, clean example of platform mortality: a decade of friendships, testimonials and community writing survived only because users had a two-year Takeout window and Google chose to keep the public community archive online. Orkut teaches the museum's central lesson — a platform can be the default social world of two enormous countries and still be deleted by a blog post.

## EXHIBIT 003 — GEOCITIES

### Verified timeline

- **November 1994** — GeoCities was started by David Bohnett and John Rezner and was **briefly named Beverly Hills Internet** before being renamed GeoCities. (Wikipedia, "GeoCities", lead and infobox: "Launched November 1994".) Computerworld independently gives "founded in 1994 as Beverly Hills Internet."
- **Mid-1990s (the neighbourhoods model)** — Users ("homesteaders") chose a themed "city"/neighbourhood for their pages, named after real places by topic: computer sites in "SiliconValley", entertainment in "Hollywood" (Wikipedia); Computerworld lists neighbourhood themes including conservation, fashion, military, sports, finance and travel. After the Yahoo acquisition, the neighbourhood URL scheme was dropped in favour of member-name URLs (July 1999). (Wikipedia; Computerworld.)
- **1998** — GeoCities was the third most popular website in the world with **more than 19 million users** (Haaretz, "Digging Up Yahoo's Graveyard"); Media Metrix data quoted in January 1999 coverage put GeoCities among the top three individual sites, with 33.4% home/work reach as of December 1998 (InternetNews).
- **28 January 1999** — Yahoo! announced it would acquire GeoCities in a **stock deal valued at around $3.6 billion** (0.3384 Yahoo shares per GeoCities share). (Contemporary reporting: Motley Fool Lunchtime News, 28 Jan 1999; InternetNews, same announcement.) Wikipedia gives the precise valuation as **$3.57 billion in stock**, with Yahoo taking control on **28 May 1999**. At the announcement GeoCities boasted **3.5 million hosted member sites** (InternetNews, citing the companies/Media Metrix).
- **April 2009** — Yahoo announced the US GeoCities service would close, and stopped new registrations; the closure date of **26 October 2009** was confirmed in June/July 2009. (Wikipedia; Computerworld; Archive Team wiki.)
- **26 October 2009** — **GeoCities (US) shut down**, with **at least 38 million user-written pages** on the service at termination. (Wikipedia.) *Nuance:* the Archive Team wiki records that pages in practice went dark around 12:30pm Pacific on **27 October 2009**, with some pages lingering for days — the "official" date and the observed death differ by about a day.
- **2009–2010 (the rescue)** — Volunteer group **Archive Team**, coordinated by Jason Scott, downloaded as much of GeoCities as possible between April and October 2009; on the first anniversary of the closure they released the rescued data as a torrent — reported as a **642 GB torrent unpacking to 909 GB** (Computerworld, "GeoCities lives — sort of") and as a **652 GB torrent** (iTnews); PCWorld rounded it to "a terabyte of data". Parallel independent archives included **Reocities** (Jacques Mattheij, ~1 million accounts and ~5 TB per Computerworld), **OoCities/oocities**, **geocities.ws** and browsable mirrors such as Geociti.es. (Computerworld; iTnews; PCWorld; Archive Team wiki.)
- **31 March 2019** — The separate GeoCities Japan service finally closed, nearly a decade after the US service. (Wikipedia.)
